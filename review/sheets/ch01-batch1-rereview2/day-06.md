## ch01.u3.s3-3.card.drum-switch

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.3 p.25 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A drum switch is a manually operated rotary switch whose handle turns a drum that makes and breaks several contact sets at once, for example to select direction.

**Drum switch**

A drum switch is operated by hand. A handle turns a drum inside the switch, and as the drum rotates, several sets of contacts open and close together according to the handle position. Each position has its own contact pattern, such as FORWARD, OFF, and REVERSE. That makes it a handy way for one person to choose between several connections with a single motion. It is a manual selector, not a sensing device: it changes state only when someone moves the handle, never because of anything the process does.

*Field note:* Check the position chart marked on the drum switch, not the handle's feel. The chart tells you which contacts are closed in each position.

## ch01.u3.s3-4.card.float-normal-state

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.4 p.26 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A float switch senses liquid level; its normal contact state is the as-manufactured state, not the state it happens to be in an empty or full tank.

**Float switch and its normal state**

A float switch senses liquid level. A float rises or falls with the surface and operates a contact at a set level. In a control schematic it is drawn in its normal state, which means the state it has with nothing acting on it: as manufactured, on the shelf. A float switch labeled normally open (NO) has an open contact when the float hangs free, whatever the tank is doing. When the float rises to its set level and the switch is actuated, an NO contact closes. The word normal never means what the tank looks like today.

*Field note:* Do not infer a float switch's contact type from whether the tank is empty or full. Read the device marking or test the contact off the process.

**Claim (what is being checked):** Normal means the shelf (as-manufactured) state; a switch can be installed so that the process holds it actuated while at rest, so its present state may differ from normal.

**Normal state versus installed state**

Normal means the shelf state, with no force or process acting on the device. Installed state means what the switch is doing right now, in place. They can differ. A switch can be mounted so the process holds it actuated at rest: a limit switch whose lever is pressed by a part that rests against it, or a float switch mounted at a height the liquid already covers. The schematic still draws it in its normal state. A normally open contact held actuated is closed now, but it is still an NO contact.

*Field note:* When a reading disagrees with the drawing, ask two questions: what is the contact's normal state, and is the process actuating it at this moment?

## ch01.u3.s3-5.card.flow-switch

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.5 p.26 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A flow switch senses moving liquid or air in a pipe or duct and operates its contact when flow reaches its set level.

**Flow switch**

A flow switch is mounted in a pipe or duct. It has a paddle or vane that sits in the stream. Moving liquid or air pushes it, and at a set amount of flow the switch actuates and changes its contact state. When flow stops, the paddle returns and the contact goes back to its normal state. Flow switches are commonly used to prove that something is actually moving, such as water through a heater or air across a coil, before another device is allowed to run. In the bucket, the flow switch lights PL7 when its NO contact closes.

*Field note:* A running pump does not prove flow. A flow switch proves the stream is there, so trace a stuck paddle or a clogged line before replacing the pump.

## ch01.u3.s3-6.card.limit-switch-mechanical

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.6 p.27 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A mechanical limit switch is actuated by physical contact with a moving part, such as a lever pushed by a machine part, and then changes the state of its contacts.

**Mechanical limit switch**

A mechanical limit switch has an arm, roller, or plunger that a moving machine part physically touches. When the part pushes the actuator far enough, the switch operates, and its contacts change state. When the part moves away, a spring returns the actuator and the contacts go back to normal. It senses position: whether a door is closed, a carriage has reached its end, or a part is present. A normally closed limit switch on a solenoid, as LS1 is in the bucket, drops the solenoid out when it is actuated.

*Field note:* A bent lever, a loose mount, or a worn roller changes where the switch operates. If a machine stops short or overruns, check the switch's mounting before changing any control logic.

## ch01.u3.s3-7.card.limit-switch-proximity

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.7 p.28 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A proximity sensor detects a nearby object without touching it; a two-wire type passes a small leakage current when off, which can hold a small, high-impedance load on.

**Proximity sensor and leakage**

A proximity sensor detects a nearby object without touching it, so nothing wears from contact. Most types are solid-state electronics, not metal contacts. A two-wire sensor is wired in series with its load, and it must draw a little current to power itself. Even when the sensor is off, a small leakage current flows through the load. A large load ignores it, but a small, high-impedance load such as a small relay or an LED lamp can be held on by that leakage. The load seems stuck on even though the sensor reads off.

*Field note:* A load that will not turn off with a two-wire sensor may be held by leakage, not by a welded contact. Check the load's size and the sensor's rated off-state leakage before replacing parts.

## ch01.u3.s3-8.card.limit-switch-optical

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.8 p.28 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** An optical (photoelectric) sensor detects objects with a light beam without touching them, either by an interrupted beam or by light reflected back from the object.

**Optical sensor**

An optical sensor uses light to detect an object without touching it. In one common arrangement, a transmitter sends a beam to a separate receiver, and the sensor operates when an object interrupts the beam. In another, one housing sends light and also receives it, and the sensor operates when an object reflects light back. It can sense objects at a distance and without wear, but dust, dirt, and misalignment can fool it. Its output is usually solid-state, and like other sensors it can be supplied with either contact arrangement.

*Field note:* If an optical sensor misbehaves, clean the lens and check alignment first. A dirty lens can look like a blocked beam or a missing object.

## ch01.u3.s3-9.card.pressure-switch

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.9 p.28 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A pressure switch senses fluid or air pressure and changes contact state at a set point; it resets only after pressure moves back past a differential. A compressor control switch opens on rising pressure at cut-out and recloses at cut-in, while a normally open pressure-proving switch such as PS1 closes on rising pressure.

**Pressure switch**

A pressure switch senses the pressure of air or liquid and changes its contact state when pressure reaches an adjustable set point. It does not reset at exactly the same pressure. There is a differential, a gap between where it actuates and where it releases, so the switch does not flutter on and off around one value. The same device is used two ways. On an air compressor, the control switch's contacts are closed at low pressure, so the motor runs, and they open when pressure rises to the upper (cut-out) setting. They close again when pressure falls back to the lower (cut-in) setting. Bucket 1A's PS1 is the other kind: a normally open pressure-proving switch that closes when pressure rises past its actuate setting and lights PL5 (AIR PRESSURE OK), then opens again only after pressure falls back to its lower release setting. Normal state again means shelf state, with no pressure on the sensing port.

*Field note:* A pressure switch holding its last state between its two settings is normal. Judge it against both set points, not one.
