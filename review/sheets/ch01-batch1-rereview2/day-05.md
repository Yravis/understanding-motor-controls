## ch01.u3.s3-1.card.auxiliary-contacts

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.1 p.23 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** Auxiliary contacts are extra small contacts operated by a device such as a contactor or relay; they carry control-circuit current and are electrically separate from the device's coil.

**Auxiliary contacts**

An auxiliary contact is a small contact that rides along with a larger device. When a contactor or relay operates, its auxiliary contacts change state too. They are built for control-circuit current, not for motor current, so they switch lamps, other coils, and logic inputs. Because they are separate pieces of metal, an auxiliary contact is electrically isolated from the coil that moves it: the coil can sit on one circuit while its contacts switch something else entirely. You will see them used to light a pilot light, to start another relay, or to feed information back to a controller.

*Field note:* When a lamp or a second relay reacts to a contactor, an auxiliary contact is usually what connects them. Check which contact is used before assuming the coil is wired to the lamp.

## ch01.u3.s3-1.card.no-nc-state-change

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.1 p.23 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A normally open contact closes and a normally closed contact opens when its operating device is energized or actuated; both return to the shelf state when it is released.

**NO and NC contacts change state together**

Contact arrangement is named for the shelf state, with the coil de-energized and nothing pressed. A normally open (NO) contact is open on the shelf and closes when the device operates. A normally closed (NC) contact is closed on the shelf and opens when the device operates. On a relay with both kinds, energizing the coil makes every contact flip at once: the NO ones close while the NC ones open. When the coil drops out, springs return every contact to its shelf state. The names describe the starting point, not the present condition.

*Field note:* When a circuit misbehaves, first ask whether each contact is in its shelf state or its operated state right now. Many wiring mistakes are an NO and NC swapped.

## ch01.u3.s3-1.card.voltage-across-contacts

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.1 p.23 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A voltmeter across an open contact in series with a load reads about the source voltage; across a closed contact it reads about 0 V.

**Voltage across a contact**

A contact in series with a load splits the source voltage with it. When the contact is closed, it is nearly a wire, so almost no voltage appears across it and a voltmeter there reads about 0 V. The load gets the rest. When the contact is open, no current flows, so the load drops nothing, and the whole source voltage appears across the open contact. A voltmeter placed across it reads about the source voltage. That is why a lamp that stays off can be traced: a reading near full voltage across a contact that should be closed means it is open.

*Field note:* Use this to find an open contact in a live circuit: across the open one you will read about source voltage; across every closed one, about zero.

## ch01.u3.s3-2.card.relay-coil-vs-contacts

*type* lessonCard · *book* 3.2 p.24 (toc-unverified) · *basis* general-knowledge · *alignment* provisional

**Claim (what is being checked):** A control relay's coil is the input that, when energized, moves an armature that changes the state of its separate contacts.

**Relay: coil versus contacts**

A control relay has two halves that are easy to confuse. The coil is the input. Current through the coil makes a magnet, and the magnet pulls an armature. The contacts are the output. The armature moves them, so they open or close other circuits. Nothing electrical connects the two halves; the link is mechanical. A coil is marked with a voltage rating and appears on the schematic as a circle with a designation such as CR1. Its contacts appear elsewhere on the drawing carrying that same designation, so you match them by name.

*Field note:* On a schematic, find the coil for CR1 first, then look for every CR1 contact. The coil rung tells you when the contacts change; the contact rungs tell you what they do.

**Claim (what is being checked):** An AC relay coil draws more current while its armature is open than after the armature has sealed in, because the air gap raises the current.

**Inrush until the armature seals**

An AC coil draws extra current at the instant it is energized. With the armature still open, the magnetic path has an air gap, and the coil has lower impedance, so more current flows. As the armature closes and seals against the core, the gap disappears, impedance rises, and the current falls to a much smaller steady value. The larger value is called inrush. Because of it, a weak supply or a long, thin control wire can sag in voltage just as a relay picks up. A relay that is held open by a jam keeps drawing inrush current and can overheat.

*Field note:* If lights dim or a relay chatters at the instant it energizes, suspect a weak source or voltage drop in the wiring rather than the relay.

An open coil is a break in the winding. The symptom is that the relay never picks up when its control circuit calls for it, even though the START button and every other device are good.
